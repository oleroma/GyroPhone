# Latency test

Two ways to see how long input takes to reach a game: **Measure buttons** on the companion's **Performance** page (its Link card; always on while switched on, no video), and the **video latency test**, a separate tool in the repo (`tools/latency-test`) that times the gyro from a slow-motion video. Measure buttons needs the Wi-Fi or USB link; Bluetooth HID has no companion to measure with.

## Measure buttons

A switch on Performance's **Link** card (off by default, saved as `MeasureButtons` in the settings). While it is on, a line under the graph shows **Buttons: X ms, phone part Y ms** (the middle of the last 64 presses and releases, how many, and the newest one).

- It times each press or release from the moment the phone sees the input (the touch event's or controller key event's own time) to the moment the PC hands the STATE carrying it to the game (SendInput and the virtual pad). The **phone part** is from the input to the phone sending that STATE: Android's input delivery, the app, and any wait a gesture makes on purpose (a When with a Held chip waits up to the Tap time to tell a tap from a hold; a tap's output comes when the finger lifts).
- It leaves out what happens before the phone sees the input (the touchscreen's or controller's own delay) and after the game gets it (the game and the monitor).
- How: the PC sends TEST_MODE with PRESS_TIMES. The phone notes the newest touch down or up, controller key edge and mouse button edge (`PressStamp`, forgotten after 1 s if nothing changed) and, after each STATE that first carries a new press or release, sends PRESS_TIME: that STATE's time and the input's time, both in the phone's clock. The PC notes when it injected each STATE (`PressLatency`), matches the PRESS_TIME to the first injection that carried its STATE (a burst injects only its newest STATE), and turns the input time into PC time with the clock offset from LINK_INFO, as the link latency does. Off, the phone sends nothing extra.

## Video latency test (tool)

Not part of the app: the phone and the companion have nothing of it, and no build, check or release includes it (`tools/` belongs to no part, and the companion's solution doesn't list it). It stays in the repo only. It measures the time from turning the phone to the game seeing the mouse move, including the screen, from a video filmed with a second phone.

**Starting it**: `tools/latency-test/latency-test.bat` builds the tool into `build/latency-test` (the .NET 8 SDK is needed; quick once built) and opens the **test window**. A video dropped on the .bat is timed in a console window instead, without the test window; `--tag <name>` marks the runs (Claude's own use `claude`).

1. **The marker image**: `tools/latency-test/markers.png` (four ArUco markers, 4×4 dictionary, ids 0 to 3, in a 16-cell grid: each marker a one-cell light grey quiet zone around its 6 × 6 black square, the squares 8 cells apart; light grey, not white, since a white screen glows on camera into the markers' thin black border). Copy it to the phone once and open it in the phone's gallery. GyroPhone must still send the gyro meanwhile: open the image in **split screen** above GyroPhone's pad (both stay active), or switch off **Send input only while the pad is open** for the test (then GyroPhone sends the gyro from behind a full-screen image).
2. **The test window**: black, with one tall green bar that moves left and right only by the mouse movement GyroPhone injects (Windows raw input with no device, which is what SendInput makes, or the input driver's own mouse, found by its ids or container id as the companion's `OwnInputDevice` does); a real mouse is ignored. While the phone twists the bar follows the mouse exactly (1:1, a fixed middle and scale): any re-centring or squeezing would shift the bar's motion in time and make the result read low. The middle and scale are set again only between takes, when the mouse has rested for 0.4 s (moved under 5% of a swing): the bar jumps back to the middle and the next take's scale fits the last take's biggest swing to 40% of the width. Where the bar would leave the window it is hidden instead of held at the edge, so those frames are left out; the hint says to rest a moment when it disappears. Esc closes it. The tool is a process of its own, so its raw input doesn't take the companion's (Diagnostics' delivery probe, "Press a key").
3. The person holds the phone in front of the test window, the marker image toward them, and twists the wrist left and right for about 10 seconds. The workspace's gyro must move the mouse.
4. They film it in slow motion (240 fps or more) with another phone from behind the hand, along the forearm, so the twist shows as the phone tilting in the picture like a steering wheel, markers and green bar in the picture, and copy the original video file to the PC (shared copies are often slowed to 30 fps).
5. They drop the video on the test window (or on the .bat). The line at the bottom of the window (or the console) shows the progress, then the result or why there is none. The video needs no trimming: walking up and putting the phone down are left out by themselves (below). The video is first copied to `last-video.<ext>` in `Documents\GyroPhone\Latency` (replacing the one before), so the last video tried can always be found again. Each result opens `results.html` in the browser.

**Analyser** (`tools/latency-test/Analysis`, OpenCV through OpenCvSharp): per frame (frames decoded in order on the CPU (the graphics card's decoder was slower and lost frames), then looked at in batches over all cores, at 640 pixels on the short side) it finds the markers and reads the phone's **tilt** within the picture (like a steering wheel; from the direction of the markers' top and bottom edges, one marker is enough). Only this one reading of the phone is used: others (its left-right position, its turn toward the camera) also pick up the hand sliding and swinging, and on one video gave answers 8 ms apart, so picking whichever matched better only hid that. It takes the green bar's left-right centre in a band of rows just above the top of the phone's marker grid (from the perspective between the grid's known layout and the markers seen), so both are read from nearly the same lines of the picture (a phone camera captures lines one after another); a frame where the bar touches the picture's edge (cut off, so its centre is wrong) counts as not found. Only the **twisting stretch** is timed: the video is cut into half-second pieces, and the longest run of pieces where both the phone and the bar are mostly tracked and the phone turns at least a quarter as fast as in its brisk pieces (one quiet piece inside is allowed) is kept; the result line says which part it timed when that isn't the whole video. Both motions are filled where tracking missed, freed of slow drift (0.6 s moving average) and scaled alike. The whole stretch's best lag (0 to 250 ms) checks that they match at all (under 0.5 the tool says why instead of giving a number) and gives the direction (it follows the mouse mapping). Then each **swing** (half-twist: from one crossing of the middle to the next, wobble under a fifth of the motion's size ignored) is timed on its own: the lag, to a fraction of a frame, where the bar best repeats that swing, scored by that swing's own correlation. Swings matching under 0.8, or with more than 5% of frames out of sight or frozen, are left out. **Freezes** are the bar standing still for 0.1 s or more while the phone moves (the motion stalls, then catches up); they are left out of the latency and reported on their own line, with the longest, since a player feels them. The **latency** is the middle (median) of the swings' lags, and the **margin** is how far that middle could be off (95%: the swings' spread, from their middle half, over the root of their count). With fewer than 3 swings timed, the latency is the whole stretch's lag over the clean frames and the result is marked **unreliable**. Frame times come from the video, so its real frame rate is used; under 100 fps the tool says why instead of giving a number.

**Results**: each one is saved as JSON in `Documents\GyroPhone\Latency` (Claude's own runs tagged `claude`), the same folder and format as when the test was part of the companion, so the history carries on. After every saved result, `results.html` in the same folder is rewritten from all of them (`LatencyReport`): one self-contained web page. It opens with a **History** chart: every run's latency over time, oldest on the left, one step per run, with a line for its margin, labelled with the app version where it changes; Claude's (tagged) runs are diamonds, unreliable ones hollow, and hovering a point names it. Every result is kept, so the chart shows how latency changes from build to build. Below it, newest first, a table (date, latency ± margin, twists, match, freezes, swings left out, video, fps, link, phone, monitor, app, tag) and one second of both motions per run as a small chart. Unreliable runs are marked. Older results (from before the swing-by-swing analyser) used an older analyser (a different spread and stretches instead of swings) and a test window that re-centred the bar, which read about 5 to 7 ms low. There is no image or data export: the page is the export. Each run records the monitor refresh rate of the test window's screen (kept in `monitor-hz.txt` for videos timed from the console) and the installed companion's version; the link and phone aren't known to the tool and show as —.

What the number includes: the phone's gyro and app, the link, the companion, Windows' desktop compositor drawing the test window, and the monitor. Rolling shutter can still leave about a millisecond or two when the camera reads the picture across its width.

**Known limits (metrics reviews of 2026-11-20; noted, nothing changed yet).**
- The test window is a WPF window. Between Windows delivering the move and light leaving the monitor come WPF's own drawing, the desktop compositor and the monitor: typically one to three screen refreshes, none of it GP's, and not what a fullscreen game has. So the absolute number is mostly the display chain.
- The printed margin covers only the scatter between swings, not fixed errors (rolling shutter, the camera's frame timing).
- It is a good **build-to-build** check on the same monitor and setup. It can't resolve link differences of 0.5–1 ms, and it doesn't measure absolute latency or compare connection modes; Diagnostics does those.
- Each result doesn't record the link mode, the phone or the build reliably (link and phone show as —), which makes its history hard to compare. Recommendations 4–7 of the 2026-11-07 review are still open.
